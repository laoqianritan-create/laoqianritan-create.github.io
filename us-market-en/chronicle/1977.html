Overview

Carter takes office · Inflation refuses to come down · Two Fed rate hikes · Dollar weakens persistently

1977 was the year in which the dollar crisis re-emerged after the collapse of Bretton Woods. Jimmy Carter was sworn in as the 39th president on January 20; the year opened with extreme cold, OPEC's two-tier price hike took effect, and a "gas shortage" in the US north idled 9,000 factories. Energy-driven price pressures pushed long rates higher: the 10-year Treasury yield jumped from 6.9% to 7.5% in January alone. The Carter administration restarted a $31 billion stimulus program, but inflation expectations had returned — the February CPI released in March was +1% MoM, an annualized 12%, re-igniting inflation fears.

On April 20 Carter unveiled the National Energy Plan; energy-industry unemployment announced in February had already topped 1 million. From May the prime rate moved up in sequence, 6.25% → 6.5% → 6.75%. In H2 the external accounts deteriorated rapidly: the current account swung from a 1976 surplus into a large 1977 deficit, with the June trade deficit at $2.8 billion alone. The Fed was forced to raise the discount rate twice — on August 29 and October 25 — to 5.75% and then 6.0%, while in late September the federal funds target was lifted to 6.25%.

After October the dollar fell sharply. Surging gold demand, a weakening currency, and rising short rates reinforced each other. With Arthur Burns's term nearing its end, Carter on December 28 nominated G. William Miller as his successor. Macro data were not especially poor — growth was decent and valuations modest — but the market was pricing a binary stagflation trap: inflation could not be tamed, and recession could not be staved off. The Dow closed at 831, -17.3% for the year, with the S&P 500 -11.50%.

Timeline of Events

General Context Significant Upward Catalyst Significant Downward Catalyst Key Historical Event (Events also marked on the chart below)
Early Jan
OPEC two-tier price hike takes effect; US "gas shortage"
Extreme cold drove energy prices up sharply; 9,000 factories in the north were forced to shut due to natural-gas shortages, and schools in several states closed for lack of heating.
Jan 20
Jimmy Carter sworn in as 39th US president
Democrats returned to the White House. After the Fed's November rate cut, the 10-year Treasury yield rose from 6.9% to 7.5% in January.
Late Jan
Carter administration restarts $31 billion economic stimulus
To offset the impact of the severe winter on the economy.
Feb 23
Fed Chair Burns testifies before Congress
Burns expressed pessimism about reducing inflation this year and warned that the widening federal deficit was weakening business confidence.
Late Mar
February CPI +1% MoM, annualized 12%
Inflation fears returned. Treasury Secretary Blumenthal had earlier called the problem "transitory."
Apr
Carter unveils the National Energy Plan
A comprehensive package of conservation, tax incentives, and price-control reform to address dependence on oil imports.
May 13
Commercial-bank prime rate raised from 6.25% to 6.5%
First hike of the year (raised again to 6.75% on May 31).
Late Jul
June trade deficit hits $2.8 billion
The external accounts deteriorated rapidly; the current-account balance swung from a 1976 surplus to a sizable deficit.
Aug 29
Fed hikes: discount rate from 5.25% to 5.75%
Followed Burns's congressional testimony, addressing external-accounts and inflation pressures. In late September the federal funds target was lifted to 6.25%.
Oct 25
Fed hikes again: discount rate from 5.75% to 6.0%
The White House had warned that higher rates would hurt the recovery; the Fed proceeded anyway. The increment was smaller than expected, and the dollar crisis intensified.
Aug
1977 Clean Air Act Amendments signed
Established the air-quality "maintenance" and "prevention of significant deterioration" frameworks and imposed stricter standards on industrial emissions and new power plants.
Dec 28
Carter nominates G. William Miller to succeed Burns
Arthur Burns's term was ending. Miller would take office in March 1978, closing out Burns's eight-year tenure.
